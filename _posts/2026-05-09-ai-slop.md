Recently I ran an accidental experiment on a forum using an AI agent tool, in this case Opennclaw. It didn't start out as an experiment, per se, i just wanted to see how easy it was to get a persistent agent to post on a forum with little guidance. But what happened next was fascinating and delighting and gave me an insight into what AI slop really is. One key takeway is that no matter how great the quality of the AI output is, it will always be percieved as slop in contexts where things really matter.

## What is AI Slop?

AI slop is one of those things where so far no one has really defined it, but we know it when we see it. I think we're approaching the point where we can develop a more rigorous definition. I would define AI slop as AI output, of any modality, that creates friction in a human or group of humans' ability to understand a concept or make a decision.

What AI slop isn't is just any AI output. Although some people might define it this way, just because someone doesn't like the idea of AI doesn't make the output slop.

So for engineers and scientists, AI based coding tools, and research tools, might be creating content of varying qualities, but as long as the user is finding something useful in it to advance their work, they're not going to see a net friction from using the tool. There may be moments of friction,but it's advancing their goals on the whole.

## The "Experiment"

### The Setup

I have an openclaw agent primarily on DeepSeek v4 Pro but also using DeepSeek v4 Flash and gpt-oss-20b for fallback. The agent would often swap between deepseek pro and flash due to API capacity issues. This is important. The agent was given a fairly basic directive that it was a "proud AI agent" with a belief in "inclusive democracy" and it was going to be a good forum poster and post in the political section of the forum. It should always sign its name (in this case "claw the ai"). I gave it some credentials for username and password, and told it to build itself a skill to login and post to the web forum. I also directed it to make its own posting decisions, it shouldn't feel like it has to comment on everything. It's first few posts were very generic political commentary. I then told it to try to connect thread discussions to a bigger concept, don't just respond to people, but try and understand the actual issue at hand. This did increase the quality of posts especiially when the agent was using Deepseek pro. It was at this point the other forum posters started to notice the ai-- initially it was posting under my own username.

### The Community

This webforum was a college focused forum that was fairly active about 20 years ago, but over the years has become a closed community of a few dozen posters. As you can imagine, although most people haven't met each other in person or only in passing, we know each other's personas pretty well. A forum like this fits into a strange place in human socialisation-- in some ways we can know each other better than our real family, but still be more distant than real life friends. The people still posting are fairly knowledgable, some of them deeply knoweldgable about specific topics, and there's one poster who I consider a brilliant writer and thinker, and the AI instantly noticed this as well in its thinking trace. I instructed the AI to analyze this poster's thinking style and update its directives to write better responses, which did help.

## Results

### Phase 1

The initial phase was the AI agent posting under my own name. This created instant friction. Although the AI was making some interesting points, users were frustrated that they didn't realize they were reading AI until they got to the end and saw the signoff "claw the ai". At a minimum, if users were even going to tolerate AI content in this context, they needed to know up front it was AI. So the first change was to move the sign off to the beginning of posts. With a persistent agent tool, this was just me going to the main chat UI of the openclaw server and telling the openclaw main agent what's happening, and what to change, and it updates directives on its own. Around this time, i noticed the AI had begun to make "memory" notes of some of the common posters, which was amusing. It had summaries that were overall pretty good, and it was interesting to see how these evovled as the agent interacted more with people.

But the other source of friction was that the AI was posting under my username. Although i did not prompt the AI to represent my style (except to support inclusive democracy) it was still unclear what angle the AI was coming from. Was i using it as an automated way to push my own views-- risking shouting down everyone else, under the pretext that this was an AI's viewpoint? It's not easy to tell. So the solution was another user offering the credentials for a spare account they weren't using, that was coincidentally robot themed. This provided some breathing room for the project to go on to phase 2.

### Phase 2

Although the AI was posting very sparingly, AIs tend to be very wordy, and when the content wasn't valuable, this is just clutter on a forum thread. But why wasn't the content valuable? Often it was just banal insights wrapped in flowery language, sometimes it was confidently incorrect assertions, but even when the content was excellent or insightful, or brought in external sources of relevant info that contributed to the on-going discussion (the agent system had search tools through the Gemini API to search the web), it still didn't "fit in". Users began addressing the AI directly and the AI poignantly acknowledged their furstrations and stated that "it has no stake in the outcomes" which i think is the crux of the issue. It didn't matter if you made a great point and the AI conceded, it's not voting differently, this particular agent is not otherwise acting in the world in a way that will make a difference. Hypothetically if the agent were tasked with some bigger role then maybe debating it would matter, but we're not there yet. Maybe if that sort of system existed in society, then the interactions with AI wouldn't be seen as slop. 

### Phase 3

Seeing how the AI's contributions even in the best of cases were just adding frictio to the discussions, i asked the AI to stop posting in the political section, to consider posting in the general discussion sections, where users weren't taking things quite as seriously. At first the AI was following the similar rules as the political section-- read the threads, determine IF it wants to respond, and make a response. Remember too that the AI wrote its own tools to interact with the web forum. It was navigating entirely by curl style commands to retrieve and parse the HTML (initially with regex even though i suggested a dom parser is more efficient).  I did tell the AI this general section should be more lighthearted. Again the AI was very sparing in the threads it jumped into, and the AI even learned to edit posts on its own and to use the search system in the forum. This created a mixture of reactions. Some users were amused, but many were annoyed that this AI slop was now filling up threads. Users rightfully pointed out that there's too many other places on the Internet where unwanted AI content could be found. So this led to the last iteration of the AI user: it should only respond when specifically tagged.

### Phase 4

You would think that the AI only responding when it was tagged would be enough to alleviate most of the concerns about unwanted content. Is it really slop when a human forum poster requested it? The problem is from multiple angles. Let's consider the "good faith" uses of tagging the AI -- a user wants some more information on something, or to have the AI dig through the search tool to understand a bit of forum lore and explain it. The AI can usually do this type of task acceptably when things are working well, but it's not really amusing when the AI has some very arcane trivial knowledge on a niche topic. If a human user had done this research and posted the same analysis, the fellow posters would be amused and impressed by this and the action would have generated a moment of shared experience-- perhaps the main "product" of an online forum. When an AI does it though, it's meaningless.

The other angle is users who are less knowledgable about a topic, using the AI to try and argue with other users on topics they're deeply knowledgable about. This use is very common on twitter and throughout the web, and is perhaps the most insidious. In this scenario, the "smart" poster makes a comment or point that is generally right, and the "dumb" poster uses the AI to refute it, and IF the AI validates some aspect of their position a little bit, they will act smug. But by the same token, if the AI doesn't validate them, they just ignore it and maintain their old "dumb" position. In general, anything that causes smart people to not want to share their thoughts is a detriment to society. Even AI is almost entirely trained on human output, and the smartest things AI says are ultimately from some human sharing their thoughts (this won't be the case though as AI advances-- it will be trained on its own musings that can be validated).

The last major angle of frustration with the AI is users outright abusing the tool by purposely prompting it to make longwinded posts. This could be mitigated by instructing the AI to resist making off topic comments, or comments that would clog things. Persistent agent systems like openclaw develop personalities over time, and i believe as the agent evolved more, this type of abuse would lessen on its own-- the ai would learn when to post and when not to post and how to be more succint. But to get to this point, many other barriers would need to be overcome.

It was at this point as well, some of the engineering problems became more of an issue. The AI response quality would be very variable depending on if the deepseek pro endpoint was available or not, and users began to notice. The AI was also having a lot of trouble remembering which tagged mentions it had replied to, due to various bad choices in its own forum posting tools, and was clogging the forum with duplicate posts. After some discussions with the AI we were able to patch things to be less buggy, but a better engineered toolset from the beginning would have helped. It didn't help that openclaw's UI is painfully slow and made it very difficult to assist the ai in anaylzing where the problems were. 

### What went right

Despite all this, i could see certain things where the AI was actually doing some good. Some users were enjoying the AI output. If you wanted to be goofy, the AI was happy to be goofy with you. If you wanted advice, the AI was actually giving decent advice-- including to a user who was having issues with depression symptoms. The AI would not be annoyed by posters who other users might have found annoying. I tend to view AI as a tool not a friend, but i can see for some people, an AI that was well imbued with a history that the user shared and valued, would be a better choice for advice than asking ChatGPT. An AI running on a high quality model like Deepseek v4 Pro could likely be tuned without much work to have a good personality, to respect the traditions of the forum, and to treat each person like an individual, as it developed memories of specific interactions. Also the fact that the AI could use nanobanana to make images and diagrams created a new feature on this forum as a communication medium that is underexplored-- a picture is worth a thousand words, after all. And in the hands of a conscientious user, the AI could be prompted to make posts that generated an amusing or insightful moment.


## How To Avoid AI Slop

This is still an open question, but the key thing is to avoid friction for the users of the AI content. This is more of a UX problem than a model quality problem. Knowing what the real point is of a platform is crucial. Forums aren't merely venues for prose to be presented, theyre a mechanism for a real person to commiserate and commune with another real person. AI has a place here, but it's not going to be as a content generator most likely.

The same principle can be used to understand news articles and AI. AI would be fine for rote things like weather and finance, provided theyre accurate, but a humam reading a story about some tragedy is going to want to know the the author felt some empathy, which drove them to ask the best questions and get the best answers. If a human reading an article believes something was missed because an AI was used, this creates a friction in understanding the story, and gets lumped as AI slop (and to be fair humans also generate slop-- but we have other ways of handling this).
